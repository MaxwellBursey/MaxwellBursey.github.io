Analog voltage reference PCB

A two-layer board that turns a 3-cell drone battery into a clean, stable 5 V reference for analog measurement.

Role
Sole designer
Timeline
January – February 2026
Tools

Deliverables

Overview

A 3-cell lithium-polymer battery is a convenient portable power source, but its voltage is anything but stable - it swings from roughly 12.6 V fully charged down to about 9 V as it discharges. Any analog measurement circuit powered directly from it inherits that drift. This project set out to design a small board that accepts the raw battery input and produces a steady 5 V analog reference that stays put regardless of the battery's state of charge.

Circuit design

The signal chain has four stages. Input protection first: a series diode arrangement guards against reverse battery connection, a realistic failure mode with hand-plugged connectors. An NCP1117 linear regulator then drops the battery voltage to a regulated 5 V rail. A zener reference network derives the stable reference node from that rail, and an MCP6001 rail-to-rail op-amp wired as a unity-gain voltage follower buffers it - delivering the reference at low output impedance so downstream loads can't pull it around. Decoupling capacitors are placed at each stage to keep the rails quiet.

Board layout

The layout is a two-layer design with a continuous ground plane on the bottom copper and dedicated power nets. I created a custom footprint for the battery connector, since the exact part wasn't in KiCad's libraries - measuring the physical part and building the footprint from its datasheet drawing. Component placement keeps the analog path short and direct from regulator to reference to output header, with test points (TP1, TP2) placed for bench verification. The silkscreen carries a proper title block: name, project, revision year.

Verification and deliverables

The schematic passed a full electrical rules check (ERC) and the layout passed design rules check (DRC) with no violations. From the finished design I generated the complete manufacturing package: Gerber files for every layer, drill files, a bill of materials with manufacturer part numbers, and a 3D model of the assembled board. The board is manufacturing-ready - the render above is the exported 3D visualization.

What I took away

This project was my first complete pass through professional PCB workflow: schematic capture, footprint creation, net management, layout under a rules check, and generating real fabrication outputs. The biggest lessons were in the unglamorous parts - library hygiene, footprint verification against datasheets, and thinking about how a human actually assembles and probes the board.
